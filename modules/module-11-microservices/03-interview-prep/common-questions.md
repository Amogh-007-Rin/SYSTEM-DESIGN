# Module 11 — Common Interview Questions

**Q1: What's the real difference between SOA and microservices? Isn't it just marketing?**
The defining technical difference is data ownership and the absence of a centralized integration broker. SOA services were often coarse-grained and frequently shared a database, integrated through a centralized Enterprise Service Bus that handled routing and orchestration — and the ESB itself often became the exact kind of complex, centrally-owned bottleneck SOA was trying to avoid. Microservices treat exclusive ownership of your own data as close to a hard rule and communicate directly (HTTP/gRPC/async messaging) with no central broker. Size isn't the defining property — independent deployability with no shared database is.

**Q2: When would you explicitly recommend *against* microservices?**
When the team is small enough that organizational boundaries don't need to be enforced by network boundaries; when domain boundaries aren't well understood yet, since premature decomposition along the wrong lines is expensive to undo later; or when the actual bottleneck is something microservices don't address at all (a single slow query is a caching or indexing problem, not an architecture problem). A modular monolith captures most of the internal-boundary discipline benefit without paying the network/operational cost.

**Q3: How do you decide service boundaries in a decomposition exercise?**
Three lenses, usually combined: by **business capability** (Order Management, Inventory, Payments — each independently meaningful to someone outside engineering, not "the validation service"); by **DDD subdomain/bounded context** (the same word, like "Product," can mean something different to Catalog vs. Inventory, and DDD says each bounded context should own its own model rather than sharing one increasingly bloated entity); and by **Conway's Law** — deliberately aligning service boundaries with team communication structure (the "Inverse Conway Maneuver"), since teams that must constantly coordinate end up coupled regardless of what the architecture diagram says.

**Q4: Why can't you just use a single database transaction across services like you would in a monolith?**
Each microservice owns its own database by design, so there's no single database engine that could enforce a cross-service ACID transaction even if you wanted one. The classic fix, two-phase commit, has its own problems at this scale: it requires every participant to hold locks from phase one until the coordinator's phase-two decision, a slow or partitioned participant blocks everyone else's locked resources, and a coordinator crash between phases leaves participants stuck unable to safely commit or abort. This is why microservices architectures use sagas and accept eventual consistency instead.

**Q5: Explain the saga pattern, and choreography vs. orchestration.**
A saga breaks one logical transaction into a sequence of local transactions, one per service, each committing independently; if a later step fails, **compensating transactions** semantically undo the already-committed earlier steps. In **choreography**, each service publishes events after its local commit and other services react — no central coordinator, but coordination logic is implicitly spread across every service's event handlers, which gets hard to debug past a few steps. In **orchestration**, a central saga orchestrator explicitly calls each service in turn and explicitly invokes compensations on failure — centralized logic that's easier to observe and reason about as the saga grows, at the cost of services being coupled to the orchestrator's calls.

**Q6: Walk through what happens when payment fails after inventory has already been reserved.**
The orchestrator (or, in choreography, the Payment service's failure event) triggers compensation for every step that already committed, in reverse order: release the inventory reservation (Inventory service runs its own local "undo" transaction, restoring the stock count), then mark the order cancelled (Order service transitions its local status). No global lock was ever held — each compensation is itself just another local transaction in the affected service's own database. **Q7: How does a circuit breaker prevent cascading failure, and what are its three states?**
Without a breaker, every caller of a struggling downstream service keeps sending requests, each one consuming a thread/connection while it waits out a timeout, until the caller itself runs out of resources and becomes unhealthy too — the failure spreads upstream. A circuit breaker tracks failures in the **closed** state (calls pass through normally); once failures cross a threshold it **opens** and fails fast for a configured reset timeout, without even attempting the downstream call; after the timeout it goes **half-open** and allows a small number of trial calls through — success closes it again, failure re-opens it. Fast-failing while open keeps callers healthy and stops adding load to an already-struggling dependency.

**Q8: What's the difference between a circuit breaker and a bulkhead, and why use both?**
They solve adjacent but different problems. A bulkhead partitions resources (thread/connection pools) per downstream dependency, so a slow or failing dependency can only exhaust *its own* allotted pool, not the shared pool every other call relies on — it limits blast radius while calls are still being attempted. A circuit breaker stops sending traffic to a dependency once it's known to be failing — it stops the attempts altogether. Used together: the bulkhead contains the damage from calls still in flight; the breaker prevents new calls from piling on at all.

**Q9: What is the strangler fig pattern, and why use it instead of a big-bang rewrite?**
Instead of a risky full rewrite-and-cutover, you route an increasing slice of traffic for one capability at a time away from the monolith and to a new microservice — typically through a routing layer/proxy sitting in front of both — until the monolith has nothing left to do and can be decommissioned. This keeps the system shippable and rollback-able throughout a migration that might take months or years, instead of betting the whole migration on one risky cutover event.

**Q10: What does a service mesh actually give you that application code doesn't already have?**
It moves cross-cutting networking concerns — mTLS encryption/authentication, uniform observability (latency, error rate, distributed tracing across every service pair), and traffic management (canary releases, retries, timeouts, circuit breaking) — out of each service's application code and into a per-instance sidecar proxy that intercepts all its traffic. Before a mesh, every team re-implements the same retry/encrypt/trace logic independently; the mesh deduplicates that into shared infrastructure, at the cost of an extra proxy hop per call and the operational complexity of running the mesh's control plane.
