# Module 20 — Common Interview Questions (Senior/Staff-Level Meta-Questions)

These are not topic questions ("what is a token bucket?") — those are covered throughout Modules 01–19 and this module's own concept sections. These are the **meta-questions** interviewers use specifically to probe senior/staff-level judgment: how you handle depth, ambiguity, and trade-off articulation, independent of which specific system is being designed.

**Q1: How do you decide how deep to go on a given component, in a 45-minute interview where you obviously can't go deep on everything?**
Go broad first — sketch every major component at a shallow level so the overall shape of the system is visible and the interviewer can redirect early if you're heading the wrong way. Then identify the 1–2 components that are genuinely hard *for this specific system* (not hard in general) and spend the remaining time there. The signal interviewers want is prioritization judgment, not exhaustive coverage — a candidate who spends 20 minutes perfecting the load balancer choice on a system whose hard part is feed ranking has misjudged where the difficulty actually lives.

**Q2: An interviewer asks "what would you do differently at 100x the scale you just designed for?" Why is this question asked, and how should you answer it?**
It's asked to check whether your design choices were actually justified by the stated scale, or just defaults you'd reach for regardless. A strong answer names the *specific* thing that breaks first at 100x (e.g., "this single Postgres primary's write throughput becomes the bottleneck, so I'd shard by [key]") rather than vaguely saying "we'd need to scale more." It also implicitly tests whether you over-engineered the original answer — if nothing in your original design needs to change at 100x, that's a sign you may have over-built for the stated scale in the first place.

**Q3: How do you handle a requirement the interviewer hasn't specified, and that meaningfully changes your design?**
State the ambiguity explicitly, propose a reasonable default with your reasoning, and keep moving — don't stall waiting for an answer to every open question, and don't silently guess without saying so. "I'll assume eventual consistency is acceptable here since this looks like a social feed, not a financial ledger — let me know if that's wrong" does both: it shows you noticed the ambiguity and it gives the interviewer a one-second hook to redirect you if your assumption is wrong.

**Q4: What does it look like to "drive the conversation" rather than just answer questions, concretely?**
State your plan before executing it ("I'll clarify scope, estimate capacity, sketch the architecture, then go deep on X"), and after finishing a section, propose what to go deep on next rather than stopping silently ("I think the interesting part here is the fan-out strategy — want me to go there, or focus elsewhere?"). Both turn you into the person setting the agenda, with the interviewer steering by exception rather than by issuing every next instruction.

**Q5: You realize, halfway through, that an earlier choice you made doesn't hold up. What do you do?**
Say so, explicitly, and revise it — "Actually, I think the single-leader replication I proposed earlier doesn't hold up given the multi-region write requirement we just discussed; let me revise that to..." Self-correction is a stronger signal than appearing to never make a mistake, because real system design involves constantly revising assumptions as more is learned, and an interviewer watching you do that live is watching the actual skill, not a rehearsed answer.

**Q6: How do you talk about a trade-off without it sounding like you're refusing to commit to a decision?**
State both sides briefly, then commit, with the specific reason tied to the stated requirements: "Strong consistency would simplify reasoning about this data, but costs a synchronous cross-region round trip on every write; given the requirement that writes need to be low-latency and a few seconds of staleness is acceptable, I'm choosing eventual consistency." That's a trade-off *and* a decision — the failure mode to avoid is listing both sides and never picking one, which reads as indecision rather than judgment.

**Q7: How should capacity estimation actually be used in the interview, beyond just producing a number?**
The number itself matters less than what it *changes* about the design — capacity estimation exists to tell you whether you need to shard, whether a single database can hold the data, whether a CDN is justified, etc. A senior answer connects the number to a decision explicitly ("at this write volume, a single Postgres primary's write throughput becomes the constraint, which is why I'm sharding by user ID") rather than producing a number and moving on without using it.

**Q8: What's a sign of mid-level vs. senior/staff-level thinking when discussing failure modes?**
Mid-level: naming generic failure modes ("what if the database goes down"). Senior/staff: naming the *specific* failure mode for the *specific* design just proposed ("if this Kafka partition's consumer group falls behind during a traffic spike, here's specifically what backs up and what the user-visible effect is, and here's the backpressure mechanism I'd add"). The specificity is the signal — it shows the failure analysis was actually performed against this design, not recited from a generic mental checklist.

**Q9: An interviewer pushes back hard on a choice you made — disagreeing with your trade-off. How do you respond?**
Engage with the substance of the pushback rather than either caving immediately or defending the original choice reflexively. If their point is valid given a requirement you'd weighted differently, say so and revise. If you still believe your choice is right given the stated requirements, restate the specific reasoning and ask what requirement they're weighting differently — disagreement is often a sign the two of you are optimizing for different implicit priorities, and surfacing that explicitly resolves it faster than repeating the same position louder.

**Q10: Why do interviewers ask about cost and operability at senior/staff level when they rarely do at mid-level?**
Because at senior/staff level, you're expected to be accountable for a system after it ships, not just for getting it past a design review — cost and operability (rollback strategy, monitoring, what an on-call engineer sees during an incident) are the parts of the job that only matter once a system is real and running, which is exactly the part of the job a senior/staff engineer owns and a more junior one typically doesn't yet. Naming them unprompted is a direct signal of that ownership mindset.
